Sunosi (solriamfetol) is a Schedule IV dopamine-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor FDA-approved for narcolepsy and obstructive sleep apnea.[1][2] Modafinil is a Schedule IV wakefulness-promoting agent FDA-approved for the same two conditions plus shift work disorder.[3]

Most comparisons stop at "both promote wakefulness, results vary by person." Three network meta-analyses, run independently in 2022, 2023, and 2025, all pooled the actual narcolepsy trials and landed on the same specific trade-off: solriamfetol comes out ahead on raw efficacy, and modafinil comes out ahead on the efficacy-to-side-effect balance.[4][5][6] That is a real, replicated finding, not "it depends."

This page is catalog and label education, not medical advice. Legal status varies by country, and Modempic does not sell Sunosi.

## The comparison, at a glance

| Attribute | Sunosi (solriamfetol) | Modafinil (Provigil) |
|---|---|---|
| Drug class | Dopamine-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (DNRI)[1] | Wakefulness-promoting agent, mechanism unlabeled[3] |
| DEA schedule | Schedule IV[7] | Schedule IV[8] |
| FDA-approved uses | Narcolepsy, OSA[1] | Narcolepsy, OSA, shift work disorder[3] |
| Not approved for | Shift work disorder, ADHD | ADHD |
| Starting dose | 75 mg (narcolepsy) or 37.5 mg (OSA), once daily[1] | 200 mg once daily[3] |
| Max daily dose | 150 mg[1] | 400 mg (no added benefit shown over 200 mg)[3] |
| Elimination half-life | ~7.1 hours[1] | ~15 hours[3] |
| First US approval | 2019[7] | 1998[8] |

Solriamfetol clears the body in roughly half the time modafinil does, yet in the trials that measured it directly, solriamfetol produced the larger wakefulness effect.[4] Shorter half-life, bigger effect. That is worth sitting with before assuming faster clearance means a weaker drug.

## A measured mechanism versus an unknown one

Modafinil's own FDA label states plainly that "the mechanism(s) through which modafinil promotes wakefulness is unknown."[3] Nineteen years after approval, that sentence has not changed.

Solriamfetol's label does not have that problem. It gives actual binding numbers: solriamfetol binds the dopamine transporter with a Ki of 14.2 micromolar and the norepinephrine transporter with a Ki of 3.7 micromolar, inhibiting reuptake of both. It has no meaningful affinity for the serotonin transporter, and none for histamine, orexin, GABA, or acetylcholine receptors.[1] The label still calls the wakefulness mechanism "unclear," but it can point to a specific, measured pharmacological action behind that uncertainty. Modafinil's label cannot.

This is not a case of "different drugs, similar vagueness." One label has numbers. The other has a 27-year-old placeholder sentence.

## What three network meta-analyses agree on

A 2022 network meta-analysis in the *Journal of Clinical Medicine* pooled 19 randomized trials covering 2,504 narcolepsy patients.[4] Solriamfetol ranked highest of all compared drugs on the Maintenance of Wakefulness Test, beating modafinil by a standardized mean difference of 0.42.[4] The same paper's safety ranking put modafinil, pitolisant, and sodium oxybate in a more balanced efficacy-safety cluster than solriamfetol.[4]

A 2023 update in *Nature and Science of Sleep*, using 17 RCTs, confirmed the pattern: solriamfetol produced the largest improvement on both the Maintenance of Wakefulness Test and the Epworth Sleepiness Scale of any drug in the comparison, modafinil included.[5]

A third network meta-analysis published in 2025 in *BMC Neurology* added a specific safety number: solriamfetol carried the highest risk of headache among the drugs it was compared against.[6]

Three independent research teams, three separate literature searches, the same two findings every time: solriamfetol wins on the wakefulness measures, and it costs more in headache risk than modafinil does. That is a specific, quantified, repeated result. Most comparison pages settle for "individual results vary" instead.

## Real patients who switched, in both directions

A 2025 retrospective chart review out of a sleep center in Sugar Land, Texas looked at 62 patients who had been on a modafinil product before their prescriber moved them to solriamfetol.[9] 71% preferred solriamfetol after the switch.

The other 29% switched back. Of those, two-thirds said the modafinil product simply worked better for them, not just that it was easier to tolerate. The remaining third cited solriamfetol side effects: anxiety in two patients, and headache, mood change, appetite loss, or a vague "felt funny" in one patient each.[9]

That is a genuinely two-sided result. Most people who try both end up preferring solriamfetol, matching the network meta-analyses. A meaningful minority do better on modafinil, and the study's own authors flagged that their patient sample already skewed toward people who had reasons to try switching in the first place.[9]

## The heart rate data behind the caution

Both labels carry cardiovascular warnings, but solriamfetol's data point is unusually specific. In a dedicated cardiac safety study, 300 mg of solriamfetol, twice the maximum recommended daily dose, raised heart rate by a mean of 21 beats per minute versus 8 bpm on placebo. At 900 mg, six times the maximum dose, the increase was 27 bpm.[1]

Those are supratherapeutic doses, not what a patient on a normal 75 to 150 mg regimen would take. But the label uses that data to justify a real instruction: check blood pressure and heart rate before starting solriamfetol and periodically during treatment, and use caution in patients with cardiovascular disease.[1] Modafinil carries a comparable cardiovascular caution, without a matching dose-response number on the label.[3]

## Three people, same question, different answer

A patient on CPAP for OSA still has daytime sleepiness. Both drugs are labeled for this, and neither replaces CPAP; both labels state the underlying airway obstruction must keep being treated.[1][3] The network meta-analyses that included OSA data lean toward solriamfetol for raw effect size, but a prescriber weighing cardiovascular history might reasonably choose modafinil's better-established safety pattern instead.[4][5]

A narcolepsy patient who has never taken either drug is starting fresh. The pooled trial data across three separate meta-analyses favors solriamfetol for wakefulness scores.[4][5][6] The trade-off is a higher rate of headache and a shorter dosing window, since the 7.1-hour half-life means timing matters more than with modafinil's 15-hour coverage.[1][3]

A shift worker needs coverage for a night shift. Solriamfetol is not approved for shift work disorder.[1] Modafinil is, dosed about an hour before the shift starts.[3] This one is not close: the label limits the choice.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Is Sunosi stronger than modafinil?

On the measures that three separate network meta-analyses tracked, solriamfetol produced a larger wakefulness effect than modafinil in narcolepsy trials.[4][5][6] It also carried a higher headache risk in at least one of those analyses.[6] "Stronger" and "better for you" are not the same question.

### Why does Sunosi work faster if it clears the body faster?

Elimination half-life measures how long a drug stays in the blood, not how strong its peak effect is. Solriamfetol's half-life is about 7.1 hours against modafinil's roughly 15, but the pooled trial data still favors solriamfetol on wakefulness scores.[1][3][4]

### Can Sunosi be used for shift work sleep disorder?

No. Sunosi's FDA approval covers narcolepsy and OSA only.[1] Modafinil is the labeled option for shift work disorder.[3]

### Is switching from modafinil to Sunosi worth it?

In one 2025 chart review, most patients who switched preferred solriamfetol, but nearly a third switched back, and most of those said modafinil worked better for them specifically.[9] There is no universal answer; it depends on which side of that split you land on.

### Do Sunosi and modafinil work the same way?

No. Solriamfetol is a dopamine-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor with defined receptor-binding data.[1] Modafinil's mechanism for promoting wakefulness is listed as unknown on its own FDA label.[3]
