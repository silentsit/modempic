## What Lyrica and pregabalin are used for

[MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/druginfo/meds/a605045.html) states that pregabalin capsules are used for neuropathic pain from diabetic nerve damage and from postherpetic neuralgia, for nerve pain after a spinal cord injury, for fibromyalgia, and with other medicines for certain partial-onset seizures.[2] The US Lyrica label lists the same five adult indications, and the seizure use also covers pediatric patients.[1]

Pregabalin is an anticonvulsant. MedlinePlus describes the action as decreasing the number of pain signals sent out by damaged nerves.[2] [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/drugs-supplements/pregabalin-oral-route/description/drg-20067411) classifies it as an anticonvulsant and neuropathic pain agent that works in the central nervous system.[3]

People still type "is Lyrica a painkiller" because nerve-pain listings sit next to opioid questions. The US label does not add a sixth use for ordinary muscle aches.

## Dosage forms on the US label

US labeling for adult indications starts at 150 mg per day, usually in two or three divided doses.[1] 300 mg per day is the labeled maximum for diabetic peripheral neuropathy pain and a common step for several other indications. Some indications allow 450 mg or 600 mg per day. The dose is cut when kidney function is reduced.[1]

A 300 mg capsule is one labeled strength. It is not "take one of these once a day." Capsules and oral solution are usually taken two or three times a day, with or without food.[2] This page does not pick a dose for you.

MedlinePlus warns that pregabalin may be habit forming. Stopping suddenly can bring withdrawal symptoms: trouble sleeping, nausea, diarrhea, headache, or seizures. The usual labeled instruction is to taper over at least one week.[1][2]

## Controlled substance, opioids, and side effects

Schedule V is the lowest US abuse schedule. It is still a schedule.

No. Lyrica is not an opioid and not a narcotic. It is an anticonvulsant. The schedule exists because abuse may lead to limited physical or psychological dependence relative to Schedule IV substances, and because some patients report euphoria.[1][4]

DailyMed lists dizziness, somnolence, dry mouth, edema, blurred vision, weight gain, and thinking abnormal (mostly trouble with concentration) as the most common adult reactions at or above 5% and twice placebo.[1] The label also warns about angioedema, hypersensitivity, suicidal thoughts or behavior, respiratory depression with other CNS depressants or in people with lung disease, and the risk of stopping too fast.[1]
